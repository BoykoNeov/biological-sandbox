Stochastic replicates, and the deterministic limit they collapse into

Three genes repressing each other in a cycle. Each run below is a Gillespie simulation of every individual reaction event — and the smooth line underneath is the ordinary differential equation those events converge to as the system gets large. Watching the first settle onto the second is what this project is about.

All of it runs here, in this tab, with no Python installed. It is the same validated code the test suite checks: the project's own wheel, running under Python compiled to WebAssembly, in a background thread.

1. Watch it run

Ω is the system size — how many molecules a concentration of 1 corresponds to. Small Ω means few molecules, so every reaction is a visible jolt; large Ω means many, and the jolts average out. Turn it down and the replicates scatter; turn it up and they close on the line.

WHAT YOU ARE SEEING the thin lines wander off, and that is the physics

Watch the left of the frame and the right. Early on the replicates sit almost on top of the smooth line. Later they are still the same shape — same height, same period — but visibly shifted along the time axis, and they drift further the longer the run goes.

That is phase drift, and it is not a defect in the simulation. Each random event nudges the oscillator slightly early or slightly late; the amplitude is pulled back by the dynamics, but nothing pulls the timing back, so those nudges accumulate like a random walk. The distance from the limit therefore keeps growing even though each replicate is a perfectly good oscillation.

This is also why this model has no single number to check it against. Its limit is a cycle, not a resting point, and an average over replicates that have drifted apart in phase flattens toward the middle — it would describe none of them. Section 3 shows the check declining to run for exactly this reason.

2. The number under the picture

The eye says the replicates hug the line more tightly at large Ω. The quantity that says it is D: the average distance between a replicate and the limit, over the whole run and all six species. Theory (Kurtz) says it should fall like Ω−1/2.

READ THIS FIRST what the button below can and cannot show

Three points, six replicates each, about a minute in total. Checked at four different seeds before being put here, it comes out in the right order every time, with the two ends separated by roughly three to five standard errors. So the direction is solid.

What it does not do is establish the exponent. Three noisy points are consistent with a range of power laws, and the middle one is not cleanly separated from either end. Showing that D falls is a different and much weaker statement than showing it falls like Ω−1/2.

The validated claim is a straight-line fit of log D against log Ω, with a statistical standard error on the slope, over many system sizes and hundreds of replicates each. It is minutes of processor time, it lives in the test suite, and it stays on a real machine. This page shows you the picture; it does not re-derive the result.

3. The check

A model in this project is finished when its closed-form predictions are reproduced by simulation, within a tolerance derived from the statistics of the measurement rather than typed in by hand. That check runs here, in the browser, on the same code.

What is not here

The heavy convergence sweeps, the full test suite, and every figure in the repository stay on a real machine — they are minutes of processor time each, and WebAssembly makes that about twice as slow. This page is the demonstration, not the laboratory. Nothing shown here is asserted anywhere; what is asserted is checked natively, and the tolerances are derived, not chosen.
